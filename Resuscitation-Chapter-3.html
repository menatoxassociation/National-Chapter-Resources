Why poisoned resuscitation is different

Standard advanced life support is the floor, not the ceiling. Four features of poisoning change what you do and, more importantly, how long you keep doing it.

The injury is reversible. A myocardium stunned by verapamil is not an infarcted myocardium. In the absence of established anoxic organ injury, the natural course of overdose is recovery once the poison is cleared by the liver, the kidney, or an extracorporeal circuit. The 2023 AHA focused update makes this argument explicitly when it discusses venoarterial ECMO: the poisoned patient can be an ideal candidate for temporary mechanical support precisely because there is something to recover to.

Physiological reserve is often preserved, though not always. Critical poisoning occurs across every age group, and a substantial share of patients — particularly in chronic cardiac glycoside toxicity, in therapeutic-error overdoses, and in deliberate self-poisoning later in life — carry significant comorbidity. What matters is not an assumption about age but an assessment of reserve: where the myocardium and the brain were sound before the exposure, the ceiling on insulin, calcium and vasopressor dosing is set by the poison rather than by the patient. Where they were not, the same aggression carries more risk and the case for early mechanical support may come sooner.

Treatment precedes diagnosis. You will stabilise most of these patients before you know the agent. History is often absent, unreliable, or deliberately false; confirmatory assays return in hours to days. Resuscitation therefore runs on physiology and toxidrome, not on a name.

The endpoints move. Ordinary resuscitation targets are calibrated to ordinary disease. In poisoning the target may be a QRS duration rather than a blood pressure, a urine pH rather than a serum bicarbonate, the disappearance of bronchorrhoea rather than a heart rate. Knowing which number you are titrating to is most of the skill.

The one habit to unlearn

Early termination of resuscitation. In poisoning, prolonged CPR, repeated antidote dosing, and extracorporeal support have produced neurologically intact survivors after arrest durations that would be futile in cardiac disease. Decide to stop on the basis of the poison's expected duration of effect, not the clock alone.

This chapter is built around the sequence you actually perform: secure gas exchange, support the circulation, control the brain and the temperature, remove what can be removed, and give the antidote that the physiology demands. The first five minutes

Six bedside data points identify almost every immediately lethal poisoning: level of consciousness, respiratory adequacy, blood pressure, QRS duration, capillary glucose, and core temperature.

Four of the six are available before any laboratory result. Get a 12-lead electrocardiogram and a glucose on every undifferentiated overdose — both change management within minutes, and both are routinely delayed while a drug screen that will not change anything is chased.

The electrocardiogram is a toxicology assay

  • QRS > 100 ms in an undifferentiated overdose should raise strong concern for cardiac sodium-channel blockade — tricyclics, cocaine, flecainide, propranolol in large doses, quinine, carbamazepine, diphenhydramine, chloroquine, bupropion. It is not diagnostic. Ventricular tachycardia, hyperkalaemia and other electrolyte disturbance, pre-existing bundle branch block, and paced rhythms all widen the QRS, and some drugs widen it by other mechanisms — bupropion-associated widening, for instance, is thought to involve impaired gap-junction conduction rather than classic sodium-channel blockade. Compare with an old electrocardiogram whenever one exists.
  • A tall terminal R wave in aVR points to the same mechanism when the QRS is only borderline.
  • Prolonged QT with a normal QRS suggests potassium-channel blockade — citalopram, methadone, antipsychotics, arsenic, many antiemetics.
  • Bradycardia with a normal QRS in a hypotensive patient narrows rapidly to beta-blocker, calcium channel blocker, digoxin, clonidine, or cholinergic poisoning — four of which have specific antidotes.

Give glucose before you interpret anything

Hypoglycaemia mimics every neurological presentation in toxicology and is produced by sulfonylureas, insulin, quinine, salicylate in children, and alcohol in the malnourished. A capillary glucose costs seconds; a missed one costs a brain.

Airway and breathing

Intubation in poisoning is rarely about the airway anatomy and almost always about the trajectory. The two errors are intubating a patient who only needed positioning and time, and intubating a patient whose own respiratory effort was the thing keeping them alive.

Deciding to intubate

Depressed consciousness alone is a weak indication. A patient with an isolated sedative-hypnotic overdose who is ventilating and oxygenating adequately, handling their own secretions, at low risk of vomiting, and on a predictable downhill-then-uphill course can often be managed with airway positioning, oxygen, suction, and continuous observation. Judge that on ventilation, oxygenation, secretion handling, aspiration risk, trajectory, and what procedures the patient needs — not on the gag reflex, which is absent in a substantial minority of normal adults and does not predict who will aspirate. Against that, intubate early and without deliberation when any of the following is present.

Choosing induction drugs

The poisoned patient is frequently hypotensive, acidaemic, and hypovolaemic before you start. Reduce the induction dose, have a vasopressor drawn up and running rather than sitting on the trolley, and correct the acidaemia you can correct before the apnoeic period rather than after it.

The salicylate ventilation trap

A patient with severe salicylate poisoning maintains a respiratory alkalosis with a very high minute volume. That alkalaemia is what keeps salicylate ionised in plasma and out of the central nervous system. Sedation, paralysis, and a ventilator set to conventional tidal volumes and rates all reduce minute ventilation below what the patient was generating. The pH falls, salicylate redistributes into the brain and heart, and arrest can follow within minutes of a technically flawless intubation.

  • Treat severe salicylate toxicity with urgent haemodialysis, not with intubation, wherever dialysis can be obtained.
  • If intubation is unavoidable: give sodium bicarbonate before induction, use apnoeic oxygenation, keep the apnoeic period as short as possible, and match or exceed the patient's spontaneous minute ventilation immediately.
  • Have the dialysis circuit being prepared in parallel, not sequentially.

Ventilating after intubation

Two poisoned groups need deliberately abnormal ventilator settings. In salicylate poisoning, match or exceed the patient's own pre-intubation minute ventilation and treat to an explicit pH target — commonly around 7.45 to 7.55, or whatever figure your local toxicology protocol specifies — with serial arterial blood gases rather than a single check. A vague instruction to keep the pH "at the alkalaemic end of normal" is how patients end up under-ventilated. In sodium-channel blockade, the same principle applies for the same reason: acidaemia increases drug binding to the channel. In everyone else, conventional lung-protective settings apply.

Respiratory failure in poisoning is not always central. Consider aspiration, which is common and frequently the reason a simple sedative overdose becomes an intensive-care admission; non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema from opioids, salicylates, and inhaled irritants; and the ascending weakness of organophosphate poisoning, in which the patient can look adequately atropinised while nicotinic paralysis progresses toward the diaphragm.

Pulse oximetry and capnography can lie

In methaemoglobinaemia the pulse oximeter drifts toward the low-to-mid eighties and stays there regardless of the oxygen delivered, while the arterial oxygen tension is normal because the problem is haemoglobin's ability to carry oxygen, not the lung's ability to transfer it. The saturation reported on a routine blood gas is usually calculated from the oxygen tension and will therefore look normal; only direct co-oximetry measures the methaemoglobin fraction. In carbon monoxide poisoning the standard pulse oximeter cannot reliably distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin and may look falsely reassuring — co-oximetry is required whenever it is suspected. In cyanide poisoning, impaired cellular oxygen extraction may raise the venous oxygen saturation toward the arterial value, but that finding is neither sensitive nor specific enough to diagnose or exclude cyanide, and treatment should never wait for it. Ask for co-oximetry whenever the clinical picture and the number disagree.

High-dose insulin

High-dose insulin euglycaemic therapy is the single most important advance in the management of calcium-channel-blocker and beta-blocker poisoning. The poisoned myocardium shifts from fatty-acid to carbohydrate metabolism at exactly the moment when calcium-channel blockade is suppressing insulin release from the pancreas and inducing peripheral insulin resistance. Supraphysiological insulin restores myocardial glucose uptake and has a direct inotropic effect independent of adrenergic receptors — which is why it works when catecholamines have failed.

The indication is not identical for the two classes, and the difference is worth holding onto. For calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, high-dose insulin is recommended for hypotension. For beta-blocker poisoning, it is recommended for hypotension that is refractory to, or given in conjunction with, vasopressor therapy. In practice both are given early in a deteriorating patient, but the beta-blocker recommendation is anchored to vasopressor failure in a way the calcium-channel-blocker one is not.

The dosing is the same: a bolus of 1 unit/kg followed by an infusion of 1 to 10 units/kg per hour, with treatment directed at avoiding hypoglycaemia, severe hypokalaemia, and severe volume overload. The commonest error is not giving too much insulin; it is stopping at an infusion rate that would be considered enormous in diabetes but is trivial here, and abandoning it before the onset of effect.

Running high-dose insulin safely

  • Use regular (soluble) human insulin. Draw the bolus and start the infusion together; do not wait for a glucose result to begin.
  • Glucose: check every 15–30 minutes until the rate is stable, then hourly. Most patients need a concentrated dextrose infusion; give it centrally when the concentration demands it. A patient who is hyperglycaemic at the outset may need no dextrose initially — but will need it later.
  • Potassium: insulin drives potassium intracellularly. Check at baseline and at least every one to two hours. Replace to keep potassium in the low-normal range rather than chasing a normal number, since total body potassium is unchanged.
  • Onset is not immediate — expect improvement over roughly 15 to 60 minutes. Titrate upwards rather than declaring failure.
  • Volume: the dextrose carrier accumulates. Use the most concentrated solution your access allows and watch for pulmonary oedema.
  • Weaning: continue while the poison is still present. Glucose requirements can persist for hours after the insulin is stopped, so keep monitoring.

Lipid emulsion is not a general rescue drug

Lipid emulsion has one strong indication and several much weaker ones, and the distinction matters at the bedside.

  • Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. Recommended, and genuinely first-line alongside airway control and correction of acidaemia. This is the only indication where the evidence is strong.
  • Sodium-channel-blocker poisoning. May be reasonable for life-threatening toxicity refractory to other treatment. The lipid emulsion workgroup positions it as a last-resort therapy in tricyclic poisoning, explicitly not as first-line.
  • Calcium channel blockers and beta-blockers — Class 2b, usefulness uncertain. This is an upgrade in 2025: the previous edition called lipid emulsion not recommended for calcium-channel-blocker poisoning and not likely to be beneficial in beta-blocker poisoning. "Uncertain" is not an endorsement — a large retrospective study found no benefit, lipid can increase gastrointestinal absorption of a drug still in the gut, and cases of abrupt collapse after administration are reported. It is never a substitute for high-dose insulin and never precedes it.

Dosing is now split at 70 kg. At or under 70 kg, 1.5 mL/kg then 0.25 mL/kg per minute for up to 30 minutes. Above 70 kg, the guideline abandons weight-based calculation entirely: a fixed 100 mL bolus over 2–3 minutes, then 200–250 mL over 15–20 minutes. All studies use 20% emulsion. If your departmental protocol still says 1.5 mL/kg for everybody, it is out of date.

Lipid interferes with laboratory assays, can clog the filters of an extracorporeal circuit, may increase gastrointestinal absorption of a lipophilic drug still in the gut, and has been associated with pancreatitis and with sudden cardiovascular collapse immediately after administration.

Refractory vasoplegia

When noradrenaline, vasopressin, and volume have not restored perfusion in a vasodilated poisoned patient, methylene blue at 1–2 mg/kg — repeated hourly if needed, with a maintenance infusion of 1 mg/kg per hour and a cumulative ceiling of about 5–7 mg/kg — has been used. Be clear about the strength of that claim: the guideline rates its usefulness for refractory vasodilatory shock from calcium-channel-blocker poisoning as uncertain (Class 2b), on the basis of case reports in which responses were mixed and sometimes transient. Its Class 1 indication is methaemoglobinaemia. Avoid or use with great caution in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency and in serotonergic poisoning, where methylene blue's monoamine oxidase inhibition can precipitate serotonin toxicity. Hydroxocobalamin has also been used for vasoplegia refractory to other measures.

Rhythm disturbance

Antidysrhythmic reflexes learned in cardiology are frequently wrong in toxicology, because the drug you would reach for often shares a mechanism with the poison you are treating.

The wide complex

A wide QRS in a poisoned patient should prompt you to treat for cardiac sodium-channel blockade, but it is not proof of it: ventricular tachycardia, hyperkalaemia, and pre-existing conduction disease produce the same tracing, and treating one does not exclude the others. Hypertonic sodium bicarbonate is the guideline-recommended therapy, and it is thought to act through two mechanisms at once — a sodium load that increases the electrochemical gradient across the channel, and alkalaemia that reduces the fraction of drug bound to it. Both physiological goals are achieved by the same drug, which is the rationale for preferring it; direct clinical evidence that it is superior to an equivalent sodium load given as hypertonic saline is not available, and its precise mechanism and optimal endpoints remain debated. What is not in doubt is that allowing the patient to become acidaemic — through hypoventilation, seizure, or under-resuscitated shock — works against the treatment.

What not to give for a poisoned wide complex

  • Class Ia and Ic agents (procainamide, flecainide) share the poison's mechanism and will widen the QRS further.
  • Amiodarone has sodium- and potassium-channel effects and negative inotropy; it is not the drug of choice here, and the AHA instead identifies lidocaine — a class Ib agent that competes for the same channel with fast off-kinetics — as a reasonable alternative or adjunct to bicarbonate.
  • Beta-adrenergic antagonists in cocaine-induced coronary vasospasm or hypertensive emergency — explicitly not recommended in 2025 (Class 3, No Benefit). Treat the cause with sedation and cooling. Note the guideline still lists calcium channel blockers among the reasonable vasodilators for cocaine-induced vasospasm.
  • Physostigmine in an undifferentiated wide-complex overdose — it is a reasonable treatment for pure antimuscarinic delirium with a normal QRS, but in tricyclic poisoning it has been associated with asystole.

Bradycardia

Atropine is worth trying and is usually insufficient in serious beta-blocker or calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, because the block is at a receptor or channel that atropine does not touch. Use standard advanced life support dosing here — 1 mg every 3–5 minutes to a maximum of 3 mg in an adult, 0.02 mg/kg to a maximum single dose of 0.5 mg in a child — and keep it mentally separate from the very much larger, doubling doses used in cholinergic poisoning. Escalate quickly to the class-specific therapy: calcium and high-dose insulin for calcium channel blockers, glucagon and high-dose insulin for beta-blockers, digoxin-specific antibody fragments for cardiac glycosides, and atropine in escalating doses for cholinergic poisoning. Electrical pacing may capture, but capture without an effective mechanical output is not a success — watch the pulse and the pressure, not the monitor.

Cardiac glycoside poisoning

Digoxin-specific immune antibody fragments reverse life-threatening dysrhythmia, hyperkalaemia, and bradycardia, and should be given on clinical grounds rather than waiting for a concentration. Reported response rates are 50% to 90%, with resolution of dysrhythmia within 30 to 45 minutes in most cases. The historical teaching that calcium causes fatal "stone heart" in digoxin toxicity is not supported by the available human data, but hyperkalaemia in cardiac glycoside poisoning is a marker of poisoning severity that is best treated by giving the antibody rather than by driving potassium intracellularly. Extracorporeal removal does not work — the volume of distribution is too large.

Note what has been withdrawn. The 2025 section on cardiac glycosides contains only two recommendations — the antibody for digoxin and digitoxin, and the antibody for Bufo toad and yellow oleander. The previous entries for atropine, electrical pacing, lidocaine, phenytoin, bretylium and extracorporeal removal are all gone. Atropine and pacing remain standard advanced life support for bradycardia and did not need a poisoning-specific recommendation; digoxin is not dialysable; bretylium is no longer manufactured. Use standard measures, and do not let them delay the antibody.

Volatile hydrocarbons: sudden sniffing death

Cardiac arrest after volatile hydrocarbon use has been called sudden sniffing death since Bass described the syndrome in 1970, and it is new to the guidelines in 2025. The mechanism is not settled, but in vitro and animal work suggests hydrocarbons sensitise cardiac potassium, sodium and calcium channels. Hypoxia, a catecholamine surge, and — with fluorinated hydrocarbons — hypocalcaemia may all contribute. In the original large series, sudden death frequently followed exertion or a frightening moment, which tells you where the catecholamines came from.

Think hard before the adrenaline

In a Queensland series of 22 patients aged thirteen and over with out-of-hospital arrest after volatile substance use, none of the eight who received adrenaline survived, while five of the six who did not receive it survived with good neurological outcome. That is a small, uncontrolled series and it does not overturn advanced life support on its own — but in a poisoning whose mechanism is catecholamine sensitisation, it is a signal pointing the opposite way to your reflex.

The guideline's single recommendation here is that a beta-adrenergic antagonist may be reasonable for ventricular arrhythmia refractory to standard therapy (Class 2b, expert opinion), on the logic that it reduces myocardial sensitivity to catecholamines. The supporting cases are mixed: one patient in pulseless ventricular tachycardia after difluoroethane inhalation survived after amiodarone, defibrillation, calcium, dexmedetomidine, esmolol and cardiac bypass; another, after inhaling difluoroethane-containing keyboard cleaner, died of refractory cardiogenic shock despite amiodarone, lidocaine and metoprolol.

If you are resuscitating a young patient found with a solvent, an aerosol or a can of keyboard cleaner, this is a conversation to have with the toxicologist while the resuscitation is running.

Torsades and the long QT

Magnesium sulfate is first-line and does not require a low magnesium concentration to be given. Correct potassium to the upper half of the normal range. If torsades recurs, increase the heart rate — isoprenaline infusion or overdrive pacing — to shorten the QT interval. Avoid amiodarone and other agents that prolong repolarisation.

Defibrillation still works

Ventricular fibrillation in poisoning is treated with standard-energy defibrillation. The difference is persistence: refractory fibrillation in a young poisoned patient is an indication to continue, to correct the underlying channel blockade and acidaemia, and to consider extracorporeal support, rather than to escalate through antidysrhythmics that may be counterproductive.

Cardiac arrest and extracorporeal support

Standard high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation remains the foundation. What changes is the reversible cause you are treating, and how long you are prepared to treat it.

The AHA is explicit that for almost every critical poisoning, the evidence is stronger for the pre-arrest state — refractory hypotension, refractory dysrhythmia — than for arrest itself. The practical implication is to treat aggressively before arrest occurs. Once arrest has happened, most antidotes remain unproven, but several retain a clear rationale.

Venoarterial ECMO

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation does for the poisoned heart what dialysis does for the poisoned kidney: it substitutes for the failing organ until the drug is gone. The AHA considers it reasonable for life-threatening shock refractory to pharmacological treatment in beta-blocker and calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, and it is a recognised option in refractory local anaesthetic and sympathomimetic cardiogenic shock. Its limits are honest ones — availability, transport logistics, comorbidity, and the real risks of cannulation and anticoagulation. It does not correct distributive shock and it does not reverse cellular poisoning such as cyanide or metformin-associated lactic acidosis.

When to make the ECMO call

Make it early and make it before arrest. The decision is a multidisciplinary one and benefits from toxicology input, because the question is not only "is this patient sick enough" but "how long will this poison last".

  • Cardiogenic shock from a cardiotoxic agent, unresponsive to high-dose insulin, calcium or glucagon, and escalating vasoactive support
  • Refractory ventricular dysrhythmia or recurrent arrest from a sodium-channel blocker
  • A young patient without significant comorbidity and without established anoxic brain injury
  • A poison with a finite, estimable duration of effect, or one that can be cleared by dialysis while on the circuit
  • Poor candidates: distributive shock alone, mitochondrial poisons, established multi-organ anoxic injury

How long to continue

There is no defensible fixed duration. Continue while there is a reversible mechanism, a treatable rhythm, or a plausible route to extracorporeal support. Published survivors of prolonged toxicological arrest are the reason this chapter exists. Involve a toxicologist in the decision to stop.

Brain, agitation, and temperature

Seizure, agitation, and hyperthermia are not three problems. They are one metabolic problem that kills through rhabdomyolysis, lactic acidosis, and disseminated coagulopathy, and the treatment for all three begins with sedation.

Seizures

Benzodiazepines are first-line for essentially every toxic seizure, and the doses required are often larger and more rapidly repeated than in epilepsy. If seizures continue, move to a barbiturate or propofol. Phenytoin has no established role in toxic seizures and is specifically to be avoided in theophylline poisoning and in sodium-channel-blocker poisoning, where it shares the poison's mechanism.

Agitation and delirium

Benzodiazepines are the mainstay, titrated to a calm patient who is breathing. Physical restraint without adequate sedation is dangerous — the AHA states plainly that prolonged physical restraint without sedation is potentially harmful, because isometric struggle against restraint generates heat, lactate, and myoglobin. Antipsychotics lower the seizure threshold, prolong the QT, and impair heat dissipation; they are a poor choice as a primary agent in toxic agitation. Ketamine and dexmedetomidine are used as adjuncts when benzodiazepine requirements become extreme.

Hyperthermia is the emergency within the emergency

A core temperature above roughly 40 °C from sympathomimetic or cocaine poisoning, serotonin toxicity, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, salicylates, or antimuscarinic poisoning is immediately life-threatening, and the rate of cooling — not merely the fact of cooling — determines the outcome.

  • Ice water immersion, 1–5 °C, is the method of choice. A systematic review of 63 studies found it faster than the alternatives, and it is preferred over other methods.
  • If ice water is unavailable, tepid water immersion at 8–17 °C or tarp-assisted ice water cooling is preferred over cooling blankets, cold packs and endovascular devices.
  • Cool at least 0.15 °C per minute. That is the explicit target — design your method around achieving it, and start cooling concurrently with resuscitation rather than after it.
  • Stop active cooling at 38.6 °C, not at normothermia, to avoid overshooting into hypothermia.
  • Sedate deeply. Muscle activity is the heat source, so sedation is a cooling intervention.
  • Paralyse with a non-depolarising agent if temperature does not fall — under sedation, with electroencephalographic monitoring if available.
  • Antipyretics do not work here; the hypothalamic set point is normal.
  • Dantrolene is not useful for life-threatening hyperthermia from causes other than malignant hyperthermia — a Class 3, No Benefit position on randomised evidence. The one place the guideline leaves the door ajar is MDMA toxicity, where it rates dantrolene as uncertain rather than useless. It is never a substitute for immersion and sedation.
  • Look for the consequences: rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, hepatic failure, and disseminated intravascular coagulation.

Serotonin toxicity and neuroleptic malignant syndrome

Both produce hyperthermia with altered mental state and abnormal neuromuscular tone, and both are treated by stopping the offending agent, cooling, and sedating. The distinction is mostly in the tone and the tempo: serotonin toxicity comes on within hours of a serotonergic drug and produces hyperreflexia and clonus that are most marked in the lower limbs; neuroleptic malignant syndrome evolves over days with lead-pipe rigidity and bradyreflexia. Cyproheptadine is an oral adjunct in serotonin toxicity; bromocriptine and dantrolene are sometimes used in neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Neither is a resuscitation drug, and neither replaces cooling.

Removing the poison

Decontamination and enhanced elimination sit below resuscitation in priority and above antidotes in frequency of misuse. Both have narrow, defensible indications.

Gastrointestinal decontamination

Should this patient get activated charcoal?

Answer the questions above.

Activated charcoal is most useful early after ingestion of a substantial dose of an adsorbable poison in a patient who can protect their airway or is already intubated. It does not adsorb metals including iron and lithium, alcohols, or corrosives. Whole-bowel irrigation has a much narrower role: sustained-release or enteric-coated cardiotoxic preparations, ingested metal such as iron or lead-containing foreign bodies, and body packers. Gastric lavage is essentially obsolete and should not be part of routine care. Induced emesis has no place.

Dermal decontamination protects the resuscitation team

In organophosphate, nerve-agent, hydrofluoric-acid, and other significant dermal exposures, remove all clothing and irrigate copiously with soap and water, with staff wearing appropriate protective barriers. Secondary contamination of emergency staff is a documented and preventable event. Decontaminate before the patient enters the resuscitation area wherever the clinical state allows it.

Enhanced elimination

Urinary alkalinisation increases the ionised fraction of weak acids in the tubular lumen and prevents their reabsorption. It is used chiefly for salicylate and for phenobarbital and the chlorophenoxy herbicides. Give sodium bicarbonate and target a urine pH of about 7.5 to 8.5, checking urine pH hourly. It fails without potassium: hypokalaemia causes the kidney to exchange potassium for hydrogen ion and acidify the urine despite systemic alkalaemia. Replace potassium aggressively, and watch for the falling ionised calcium.

Is this poison dialysable?

Select a poison.

Dialysability depends on molecular size, protein binding, volume of distribution, and endogenous clearance. A poison with a large volume of distribution — tricyclics, digoxin, most calcium channel blockers — cannot be meaningfully removed no matter how long the circuit runs, because the plasma compartment holds almost none of it.

9

Cellular poisons: cyanide and methaemoglobinaemia

Two poisonings in which the patient is dying of impaired oxygen use rather than impaired oxygen delivery, and in which the antidote is the resuscitation.

Cyanide

Cyanide blocks the mitochondrial electron transport chain, so aerobic respiration and adenosine triphosphate production stop while arterial oxygen content stays normal. The clinical result is cardiovascular collapse, profound lactic acidaemia, depressed consciousness, seizures, and death. Confirmatory blood cyanide measurement is almost never available in clinical time, so the decision to treat is a clinical one.

Treat on suspicion

Do not wait for a confirmatory test. Treat empirically in the patient pulled from a structure fire, the laboratory or industrial worker, and the deliberate ingestion, who presents with cardiac arrest, altered mental status, a high plasma lactate, severe metabolic acidosis, or hypotension. Cyanide and carbon monoxide poisoning frequently coexist in smoke inhalation.

  • Hydroxocobalamin is first choice (Class 1). It scavenges cyanide on an equimolar basis to form cyanocobalamin, acts rapidly, and — unlike sodium nitrite — does not cause hypotension or reduce oxygen-carrying capacity, which is why it is preferred in smoke inhalation.
  • Sodium nitrite together with sodium thiosulfate is recommended when hydroxocobalamin is unavailable (Class 1). Note the 2025 wording: it is a combination recommendation, not nitrite alone. Nitrite induces methaemoglobinaemia, which binds cyanide — and which is exactly why it is a poor choice alongside carbon monoxide poisoning. Paediatric and anaemic dosing must be precise.
  • Where carbon monoxide and cyanide coexist — the house fire — and hydroxocobalamin is unavailable, give sodium thiosulfate alone (Class 2a). This is the 2025 answer to the old dilemma of not wanting to add methaemoglobin to carboxyhaemoglobin in a patient whose oxygen-carrying capacity is already compromised.
  • Adding sodium thiosulfate to hydroxocobalamin may be reasonable (Class 2b, downgraded in 2025). It donates sulfur for the conversion of cyanide to thiocyanate, but is slow.
  • 100% oxygen remains standard practice on the logic that the defect is cellular oxygen use, though the 2025 section no longer carries a separate graded recommendation for it.
  • Expect hydroxocobalamin to turn skin, urine, and plasma deep red, and to interfere with colorimetric laboratory assays and with co-oximetry. Warn the laboratory before the samples arrive.

Methaemoglobinaemia

An oxidant stressor converts haem iron from the ferrous to the ferric state, and ferric haemoglobin neither binds nor delivers oxygen. Nitrates and nitrites, dapsone, benzocaine and prilocaine, phenazopyridine, and aniline compounds are the usual culprits. Moderate concentrations are tolerated; severe methaemoglobinaemia produces cardiovascular collapse.

The saturation gap

The patient is dusky and breathless, the pulse oximeter reads in the low-to-mid eighties and refuses to move with supplemental oxygen, and the arterial blood gas reports a normal oxygen tension with a normal calculated saturation. That discrepancy — a measured oximeter saturation far below the saturation calculated from the gas — is the diagnosis until co-oximetry confirms it.

  • Methylene blue is the treatment (Class 1), acting as a cofactor for the reduction of methaemoglobin back to haemoglobin.
  • Exchange transfusion (Class 2b) and hyperbaric oxygen (Class 2b) may be reasonable when methylene blue fails, though the fall in methaemoglobin with hyperbaric therapy can take hours and it is impractical during cardiopulmonary collapse.
  • N-acetylcysteine is not recommended (Class 3: No Benefit) — a blinded crossover volunteer study found no reduction in nitrite-induced methaemoglobin.
  • Ascorbic acid may be reasonable only when methylene blue is contraindicated or unavailable (Class 2b — upgraded in 2025 from not recommended, and the qualifier is the whole point). It acts slowly and needs repeated doses over hours, so it will not rescue a collapsing patient. In G6PD deficiency, where methylene blue may fail and may cause haemolysis, this is the situation the recommendation was written for.

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency

Methylene blue may fail to reduce methaemoglobin in G6PD deficiency and can precipitate haemolysis. Enzyme activity testing is almost never available in real time, so the decision is clinical. This matters more here than in most of the world: G6PD deficiency is common in parts of the Arabian Peninsula, and a patient's own or family history of haemolysis after fava beans, sulfonamides, or naphthalene should shape the plan before the drug is drawn up. Where methylene blue is contraindicated or ineffective, exchange transfusion and hyperbaric oxygen are the fallbacks.

What the guideline recommends, and how strongly

Toxicological resuscitation runs on thin evidence, and the grading system is the honest record of how thin. Only 2 of the 235 recommendations in Part 10 are supported by Level A evidence — and both of them are in this chapter's territory: give atropine immediately in organophosphate poisoning, and pralidoxime is reasonable in adults. Everything else you do to a poisoned patient rests on weaker ground. Knowing which is which changes how hard you should argue for a therapy at three in the morning.

The full breakdown, for the whole of Part 10: 2 recommendations rest on Level A evidence, 9 on randomised Level B, 32 on non-randomised Level B, 67 on limited data and 125 on expert opinion alone. By strength, 66 are Class 1, 84 Class 2a, 58 Class 2b, 21 Class 3 No Benefit and 6 Class 3 Harm.

This is not a criticism of the guideline; it is a description of the field. Cardiac arrest from poisoning is rare outside opioid overdose, the cases are heterogeneous, case reports are subject to publication bias, and randomising critically poisoned patients — many of whom have attempted self-harm — raises real ethical difficulty. The writing group says so explicitly. The practical consequence is that a Class 1 recommendation built on limited data still means "do it", but it also means the next case series could move it.

What changed in October 2025

This chapter follows Part 10 of the 2025 guidelines, which superseded the 2023 focused update on 22 October 2025. If your departmental protocol was written against the 2023 document, these are the changes that invalidate parts of it:

  • Pralidoxime — the adult dose is now 2 g infused over 15–30 minutes, explicitly not bolused, with maintenance raised from 400–600 mg/hour to 1 g/hour.
  • Lipid emulsion — dosing is split at 70 kg, above which weight-based calculation is abandoned for a fixed 100 mL bolus and a 200–250 mL infusion.
  • Lipid emulsion in calcium-channel-blocker and beta-blocker poisoning — upgraded from "not recommended" and "not likely to be beneficial" to "usefulness uncertain". Not an endorsement.
  • Calcium — now also recommended in beta-blocker poisoning.
  • Sodium bicarbonate — local anaesthetics added to the indication; hyperventilation alongside bicarbonate is now a recommendation in its own right.
  • Methylene blue — a single 7 mg/kg ceiling, replacing 5–7 mg/kg.
  • Ascorbic acid in methaemoglobinaemia — from "not recommended" to "may be reasonable, only where methylene blue is contraindicated or unavailable".
  • Cyanide — nitrite and thiosulfate are now recommended as a combination; thiosulfate alone is the answer where carbon monoxide coexists and hydroxocobalamin is unavailable.
  • Cardiac glycosides — the atropine, pacing, antiarrhythmic and extracorporeal-removal recommendations were all withdrawn, leaving only the antibody.
  • Cocaine — beta-adrenergic antagonists in vasospasm or hypertensive emergency are now explicitly not recommended.
  • Opioids — an antagonist during cardiac arrest moved from "no known benefit" to "may be reasonable, provided it does not interfere with CPR".
  • Hyperthermia — ice water immersion named as the preferred method, a cooling rate of at least 0.15 °C per minute specified, and a stopping point of 38.6 °C.
  • Volatile hydrocarbons — an entirely new section.

Separately, to keep grading consistent across the whole document, many recommendations were reassigned to expert opinion. A recommendation can therefore appear to have weakened without any new evidence arriving.

Reading the classes

Class 1Strong. Benefit greatly outweighs risk. Do it.
Class 2aModerate. Reasonable to do; benefit outweighs risk.
Class 2bWeak. May be reasonable; usefulness uncertain.
No benefitNot recommended; unlikely to help.
HarmPotentially harmful. Do not do it.

Level of evidence is separate from class. A means high-quality randomised data; B-R and B-NR are moderate randomised and non-randomised; C-LD is limited data; C-EO is expert opinion alone.

Where the guideline stops

The writing groups name their own gaps, and they are the questions you will actually face. What is the best empirical dose of digoxin antibody in arrest? Which patients with organophosphate poisoning benefit from an oxime, which oxime, and at what dose? What physiological or electrocardiographic target should you treat to in sodium-channel blockade to prevent arrest, rather than treating arrest once it happens? Is the optimal treatment of non-bupivacaine local anaesthetic toxicity the same as for bupivacaine? Does gastrointestinal decontamination help in life-threatening beta-blocker or calcium-channel-blocker poisoning, particularly with extended-release preparations? What is the optimal timing of ECMO — periarrest, or earlier? When you find yourself without an answer in one of these areas, it is because there is not one. Call the toxicologist, document your reasoning, and publish the case.

Local readiness

Nothing in this chapter works if the antidote is not in the building. Antidote stocking is a resuscitation intervention, and it is the one that has to be done before the patient arrives.

Audit your own department against the list below. For each item the questions are the same: do we stock it, how much, where exactly is it, how long does it take to reach the resuscitation bay, and who can authorise release out of hours?

Antidote stock audit

  • Immediately available in the resuscitation area: sodium bicarbonate, calcium chloride or gluconate, atropine, naloxone, glucose 50%, regular insulin, magnesium sulfate, benzodiazepines, 20% lipid emulsion
  • Available within minutes from pharmacy: glucagon, methylene blue, hydroxocobalamin, sodium thiosulfate, pralidoxime, digoxin-specific antibody fragments, fomepizole or ethanol, pyridoxine, N-acetylcysteine, deferoxamine
  • Regionally held with a known transfer pathway: antivenoms, large-volume digoxin antibody stock, dimercaprol and succimer, physostigmine
  • Quantities matter more than presence. A single vial of hydroxocobalamin or two vials of digoxin antibody is a stocking failure dressed as a success. Stock enough for one severe adult case, not one dose.

Systems that shorten the interval

  • A written, laminated antidote location map in the resuscitation room
  • Weight-based dosing sheets prepared in advance for the high-acuity antidotes — insulin, bicarbonate, calcium, lipid emulsion
  • A standing arrangement with the dialysis service for out-of-hours emergency haemodialysis, with salicylate and toxic alcohol poisoning named explicitly
  • A named route to extracorporeal life support and the time it actually takes, measured rather than assumed
  • A poison-centre or clinical-toxicology number displayed where the team can see it, and used early

Scope and use

This chapter is a teaching and decision-support resource for clinicians. Doses are those commonly reported in the resuscitation literature and are starting points for titration in a critically poisoned patient, not prescriptions. Every calculated dose should be checked against the concentration of the preparation in your own department and rounded to a volume your equipment can deliver. Local protocols, product formulations, and the advice of a clinical toxicologist take precedence.

Recommendation classes quoted in this chapter are those of Part 10 of the 2025 guidelines. Where a class differs between the adult and paediatric recommendation, the adult grading is given and the difference noted.
